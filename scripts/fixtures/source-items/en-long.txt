The printing press, introduced to Europe by Johannes Gutenberg around 1450, replaced hand copying with movable metal type. Before it, a single book could take a scribe months to produce, so books were rare and expensive. Movable type made it possible to reuse the same letters for any page, which cut the cost of each additional copy sharply.

The spread of cheap books changed who could read. Literacy rose in the cities first because merchants and craftsmen needed written records, and printers found their largest market among them. Over several generations, vernacular languages replaced Latin as the main language of printed books, since printers sold more copies in languages ordinary people spoke.

Printing also fixed spelling and grammar. Because thousands of identical copies circulated, regional variants of a language slowly converged on the forms used by the largest printing centres. Dictionaries and grammar guides appeared in the sixteenth century to record these standard forms.

The press helped the Reformation spread. Martin Luther's theses and his German translation of the Bible were printed in large numbers within a few years, so his ideas reached readers faster than church authorities could suppress them. Pamphlets, cheap and short, became the main tool of religious and political argument.

Scientific knowledge changed as well. Printed tables of astronomical data allowed scholars in different countries to compare identical numbers, and errors that had been copied silently by scribes could now be noticed and corrected in later editions. Anatomical atlases with engraved illustrations let students study the same image of the human body regardless of where they lived.

Governments reacted with licensing and censorship. Authorities required printers to obtain permission before publishing and compiled lists of forbidden books. These controls were difficult to enforce because a banned text could be reprinted in another city or country, so printers in the Dutch Republic became known for publishing works that were illegal elsewhere.

The economics of printing created new professions. Publishers financed editions, booksellers distributed them across trade routes, and proofreaders checked the type before each run. Copyright did not exist yet, so a successful book was often pirated by rival printers within weeks, which led authors and printers to seek royal privileges that protected a title for a fixed number of years.

Newspapers grew out of the same technology. Weekly news sheets appeared in the early seventeenth century, collecting reports from merchants and diplomats, and became a regular source of information about prices, wars and politics for the urban public.

Printing changed education too. Schools could afford identical textbooks for every pupil, so teachers stopped dictating lessons and began assigning pages to read. Standard textbooks made it easier to compare what students in different towns learned, and universities began to examine students against the same printed texts.

Maps and navigation charts benefited from the press. Engraved plates produced many identical copies of a coastline, so pilots could share corrections and update later editions. Accurate printed maps encouraged longer voyages because captains trusted the same charts that other captains had already checked.

Not every effect was positive for existing trades. Scribes and manuscript illuminators lost much of their income, although some moved into engraving and decoration for printed books. Guilds of copyists petitioned city councils to limit printers, but the lower price of printed books meant that demand grew faster than the old trade could ever have supplied.

Libraries changed their purpose. Before printing, a library was a locked room of a few hundred chained manuscripts kept for a handful of scholars. Once printed books became cheap, private collections grew, and public libraries appeared in the following centuries because ordinary towns could afford to buy and lend thousands of volumes.
